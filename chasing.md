# Getting Vendors to Respond

> [!NOTE]
> Draft. Not yet reviewed.

**Purpose.** The part nobody writes about. You sent the questionnaire and nothing came back, and this, not the framework, is where programs actually die. The follow-up email nobody sent has killed more TPRM programs than every breach combined.

## Who to Send It To

Not your account rep. Their job is renewal, and your questionnaire is friction on the path to it, so it goes in a drawer. Ask the rep one question instead: who owns security questionnaires at your company, and send it there directly, with the rep copied so the commercial relationship knows it exists. Bigger vendors have a trust portal with half the answers pre-published; take the portal answers and send only the delta, which shrinks your ask and signals you did the reading.

## Timing Against Renewal

Renewal is the only leverage you have, so schedule the program around it: assessments go out 90 days before renewal, not on the anniversary of the last one. A questionnaire with a purchase order behind it answers itself. The same ask in the dead middle of a three-year term is a favor request, and it gets favor-request treatment. Where the register shows a critical vendor with a distant renewal, use the other lever: new business. The moment they want to expand the contract is a moment they answer questions.

## The Escalation Ladder

Four rungs, one week apart, each one sentence longer than polite. The reminder, to the same contact. The re-send, rep copied, deadline restated. The commercial nudge: your rep hears "we cannot renew without this on file," which converts your problem into their commission's problem, and their commission is a highly motivated colleague. The executive step, critical vendors only: your sponsor emails their counterpart, once, short. Almost nothing survives rung three, because rung three is the first one where somebody at the vendor is paid to care.

## When a Vendor Refuses

A flat no is information, and better information than most completed questionnaires. Ask for the nearest alternative once: the SOC 2 instead, the trust portal, a 30-minute call in place of the form. Real security teams counter-offer, because they have something to show. A vendor with no questionnaire, no report, no portal, and no call to offer is showing you the program they run, and the [scoring model](scoring.md) has a color for that.

## Escalating Internally Instead

At some point the chase stops being a vendor problem and becomes a business decision, and the trick is noticing the handoff moment. Go to your business owner with three sentences: here is what we asked, here is the silence, here is what we are trusting them with anyway. Sometimes the owner has a relationship that unlocks it in one phone call. Sometimes they shrug, and the shrug is the real answer, which routes to [risk acceptance](risk-acceptance.md) with the shrugger's name on the form. Either way, you stopped being the program's collection agency, which was never the job.

## When to Accept and Move On

Set the cap up front: three chases and an internal escalation, then the file closes one of two ways, an acceptance signed by the business owner, or a replacement conversation at renewal. Write the outcome down and stop. An honest register that says "asked four times, no answer, risk accepted by name, revisit at renewal" is a functioning program. Seventeen months of unanswered reminders in an inbox is not diligence, it is evidence of diligence theater, and it reads exactly that way to the regulator, the insurer, or the plaintiff who eventually asks.
